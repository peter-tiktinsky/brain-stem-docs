Surface · from idea to done

Plans

A plan is how a multi-step effort is stored on disk and shepherded from a raw idea to a finished, independently checked, signed-off result. The whole tree lives in ~/.claude-plans, a deliberate parallel to Claude Code's own plans folder, with a structure chosen against researched best practices. That one relocation is what makes the funnel, the binder, and the orchestrator possible.

Surfaced in the vault as Plans/; rolled up per project into Projects/.

Why a parallel plan tree — and why it's concrete

Claude Code treats its own config folder (~/.claude/**) as sensitive and guards it — writes and edits there fail unreliably. A plan tree has to be written to constantly: re-rendering tasks.md, regenerating indexes, scrubbing research into the Library. A store you cannot reliably write to is not a store. The vault itself is no good either — files in a sync boundary fail silently. So the tree lives outside the guarded folder, at ~/.claude-plans, and a single Plans/ symlink makes the whole thing browsable in your notes app.

This is not an ad-hoc convention. Brain Stem researched best practices in plan organization and execution and adopted a structure that converges with the field's own answers (the evidence is in the next section). Native Claude Code contributes almost nothing here on its own — no enforced quartet, no lifecycle, no source-of-truth control file, no funnel, no binder, no orchestrator. Plans is the one pillar Brain Stem adopts as a methodology rather than receives as a platform feature, and the parallel folder is what gives that methodology somewhere to live.

Moving the tree did more than find plans a writable home — it made their universality usable. Plans are naturally a cross-project category: a plan isn't bound to one repo or one project's notes the way most artifacts are — you accumulate them across everything you work on. Native Claude Code leaves that potential stranded inside its guarded config folder; Brain Stem gathers every plan into one governed, writable tree. That turns a naturally universal category into a deliberate universal asset layer — one governed place, spanning all your projects, that you can lean on broadly: capture and triage ideas, accumulate researched plans, and reach back for past work wherever you launch. The two roll-ups below are the first thing that layer makes possible.

The same folder setup powers two consolidations

Because the plan tree is a writable, governed, plain-text store, Brain Stem can roll it up two ways at once. Per-project binders (Projects/) consolidate one project's context across all its plans. And the one store Claude maintains universally across every project — the Library (Wiki/) — consolidates reusable context promoted out of those plans. (There is no separate "universal binder"; the universal store is the Library.) Binders for project context, the Library for universal context, both derived from the same tree.

The quartet — and why it's best-practice

Each plan is a numbered folder holding four files with sharply separated jobs (plus an optional fifth, the ideation brief — the "Session 0" research record).

FileJobOwner
spec.mdThe goal and constraints — the north star. Fixed once set, and capped at 1100 lines.Human-authored
manifest.jsonThe source of truth — status plus the structured task list, schema-validated.Machine-validated
tasks.mdThe human-readable to-do — a generated render of the manifest, between sentinel markers.Machine-rendered
handoff.mdAppend-only, newest-first session diary; its Next session: line carries the resume.Appended per session
The manifest is the spreadsheet; tasks.md is its export

Because tasks.md is generated from the manifest between sentinel markers, hand-edits inside those markers are overwritten on the next render. Change status in the manifest; read it in tasks.md. As the design doc puts it: done is recorded in the spreadsheet, not penciled onto the printout.

Why this shape — the evidence

None of this is a private invention, which is the strongest signal it's right. The forgeable-checkbox failure (an agent claiming it ticked a box it didn't) and the up-only reporting rule both track the documented failure modes of unsupervised multi-agent systems (a published multi-agent failure taxonomy). The claim-versus-independent-check distinction is standard project-management practice (the PMI / PMBOK separation of verified from accepted deliverables): a plan’s record is a claim until an independent read-only pass checks it against the artifacts. And a structured source of truth rendered into a human list — not the reverse — is the lesson agentic-coding tools settled on independently (the tasks.json-style pattern). The design converges with those external answers rather than departing from them.

Anatomy of a plan folder

The quartet is the core of a plan folder, not all of it. A plan folder has a fixed managed set of twelve names: the entries the plan tools own, each with one job and one writer. The table lists all twelve.

Anything else at the top of an active plan folder, such as a staged site or a scripts directory, draws a plan-unmanaged-entry finding from the placement sweep. Sub-plan folders and dotfiles are exempt, and a completed or superseded plan is never swept. The fix is to record the entry in the manifest’s assets[] list, one row of path and purpose, with /librarian plan-asset add <plan-dir> <path> --purpose "<text>". That command refuses a name already in the managed set, a path of more than one segment, a path that does not exist and an empty purpose, and a refusal writes nothing.

Gate records and the _gates/ folder

_gates/ is a folder convention: one flat folder per plan for the records of whatever review you run on it. A record is named NN-gate<k>-<kind>.md. NN orders the records, k is the gate number (1 or 2) and kind names the record, for example charge, round1-record or verdict. A write into _gates/ whose name misses that pattern is allowed, with a [GATE RECORDS] advisory that states the pattern.

The folder exists so that review records never count as research. Declared research feeds the binder’s research index and the Library’s promotion, and a review record in either would be noise. The write guard therefore stops two kinds of new file in _research/. A file with a gate-named name, one whose name carries a token such as charge, verdict, gate1, gate2 or round plus a number, is denied with [GATE RECORD], and the message names the _gates/ path to write instead. A file whose NN- number prefix another file in the folder already carries is denied with [RESEARCH PREFIX], and the message names the next free prefix. An existing file and an edit are never denied. The guard sees only the Write tool. A shell write bypasses the guard. When one puts a gate-named or prefix-colliding file in _research/, the session-close declare step skips it and warns, so the file is never declared as research.

No Brain Stem command writes into _gates/, and a new plan does not start with one. The records come from your own review process: the folder and the guard only keep them in one predictable place, out of the research. Whether a plan needs gate records at all is your call.

The two pointer files

Two small files say which plan is under build. .active-plan sits in the project’s binder directory (see Arming a plan for build below) and holds one line: the folder name of the armed plan. .active-sp sits inside a master plan and holds one line: the folder name of the armed sub-plan. Whitespace is ignored. For .active-sp, a single dot, an absent file or a name that matches no folder all mean the plan root. On an installed system, no Brain Stem command writes the pointer files, so you write both pointer files yourself.

Two folders named deliverables/

The deliverables/ folder in the table is inside a plan folder, and it holds documents the plan produces for its own record. It is not the Work/<project>/deliverables/ folder, where a project keeps finished work for a client or an audience; that one belongs to the project’s work area and is described in the work-folder guidebook.

Beyond the quartet: research and decisions

A plan folder gives the quartet two governed companions — used both before execution (during planning) and during execution at human gates:

  • Research — _research/. Durable findings, each declared in the manifest's research_artifacts[] (with a status of active, finalized, deferred or superseded). The folder holds the curated canonical set: gate records and prefix-collision files live in the plan's _gates/ folder, never in _research/. A finalized finding is the stock the Library promotes from, and every finding rolls into the binder's research index, canonical findings first and superseded ones after, each forward-linked to its replacement. A replaced finding is marked with plan-research-supersede, and nothing is deleted or renamed; Research, start to finish walks the whole path.
  • Decisions — the ADR ledger. A per-plan decisions/ folder of immutable decision records (each Context · Decision · Consequences), declared in decision_records[]. A decision never changes in place — a new ADR supersedes the old one, forward-linked. On resume, Claude reloads the non-superseded ADRs straight from the manifest pointer, not from spec archaeology. An accepted ADR is locked: an edit to its body above the first ## Amendment heading is denied with [ADR LOCK], which names the two routes — append an amendment section, or run /librarian adr-supersede <plan-dir> <old-ADR> <new-ADR>, which marks the replaced ADR superseded in its Status line, its frontmatter and the manifest's decision_records[].

Research, start to finish

A finding travels one path, in a fixed order: staged, written, declared, finalized, replaced if a later finding supersedes it, indexed and, when it earns it, promoted to the Library. Each step below says what runs, when, and what it writes.

  1. Stage it, if it is not ready for the plan. Precursor research can wait on a Workshop bench, a scratch folder outside the plan: /librarian workshop open <key> creates it. workshop promote then moves exactly one chosen file into the plan’s _research/ under the next free NN- prefix and archives the rest of the bench. workshop promote refuses a gate-named file and points at _gates/.
  2. Write it into the plan. A finding is a Markdown file in _research/. The write guard acts at write time: it denies the write of a new file that is gate-named or whose NN- prefix another file in the folder already carries, and its message names the _gates/ path or the next free prefix. Only the Write tool is guarded, and an existing file or an edit is never denied.
  3. Declare it at session close. plan-research-declare scans _research/, decisions/, target-state/ and deliverables/ for Markdown files the manifest does not yet list, and appends one research_artifacts[] entry for each: an id of the form RA-NN, the file’s first heading as the title, the status active and the path. It never rewrites or removes an entry you curated. A gate-named or prefix-colliding file that reached _research/ by a shell write is not declared: the declare step skips it and warns. A plan whose manifest carries research_closed stops declaring new files on its own. A new file draws a warning for you to adjudicate, the manifest is left untouched, and the next session start shows the warning again. The flag is set when a plan reaches a terminal status, and you can set it by hand on an active plan.
  4. Mark it settled, by hand. When a finding is settled, change its status to finalized in the manifest yourself, because nothing sets it for you. Only finalized entries are promotable; a deferred or superseded entry is passed over.
  5. Replace it, if a later finding supersedes it. Run plan-research-supersede with the plan folder, the replaced id and the replacing id. It sets the replaced entry to superseded with a superseded_by link to the new id, gives the new entry a supersedes link back, and inserts a one-line banner naming the replacement into the replaced file. Nothing is moved or deleted. The command refuses an id the manifest does not list, a finding replacing itself, an entry that is already superseded and a loop, and a refusal writes nothing.
  6. Index it. plan-research-index rebuilds the binder’s research index from the manifests, one row per declared finding with a link that resolves to its file. Canonical findings come first, then the superseded ones, each forward-linked to its replacement. The project binder page shows where the index sits.
  7. Promote it, if it deserves a wider life. Promotion into the Library is a separate step that proposes before it writes. library-scrub turns a finalized finding into a universal article and writes only on --apply; --census lists the research of completed plans that has not been promoted yet. A queue mode offers each newly completed plan’s research for promotion, switched by library_promotion in your user manifest: prompt, auto or off, and off when unset. Dual context layers covers promotion in depth.

Stage one — the planning lifecycle (idea → formal plan)

Not every idea deserves a numbered plan on day one. The funnel keeps capture cheap and spends structure only on ideas that earn it. A number is never burned until graduation.

Capture & triage — an idea note (/backlog-triage) is classified against what already exists, so duplicates and overlaps are caught before they become plans.
Research-first — /backlog-research reads your vault, your tools, and outside best practices, then writes the ideation brief and a recommendation.
Graduate — /new-plan (or research's --promote) scaffolds the numbered folder through one shared mechanical path; the idea note is retired.

Ideas carry their own short status — new → triaged → briefed — a separate vocabulary from a plan's lifecycle. The backlog itself is generated and owned by the librarian: the funnel skills only edit the underlying idea notes, and the table is re-derived from them. The human gates here are the classification call at triage and the go/no-go on the research recommendation.

Not every idea graduates — and the funnel is honest about the ones that don't. When a note reaches a terminal resolution instead (done another way, folded into an existing plan, dropped on purpose), it is stamped with that resolution and moved out of the inbox into a _settled/ subfolder, so the inbox holds live ideas only. A note settled by your judgment rather than by machinery goes through the librarian's inbox-settle capability — dry-run by default, strict resolution vocabulary, and it refuses to settle the same note twice. When a plan absorbs several notes, the plan's manifest records the absorbed notes from its side too, so the settled ledger can derive the whole story from three sources: graduations, absorptions, and terminally-resolved notes. A capture is decided when it is made: STANDALONE (with a release class and a size), ABSORB (into a named plan) or NO-BUILD, a decided no-for-now that names a registered re-open gate and comes back into the inbox when the backlog index finds the gate met. Classifying a note at triage is one command, triage-apply.sh, and a parked NO-BUILD note whose gate is manual is re-opened with reopen-idea.sh.

Stage two — preparing the plan

A freshly graduated plan is scaffolded but thin. Preparation is where you and Claude flesh it out, and the file ownership stays strict the whole way:

  • You author the spec — the goal and constraints, fixed once set. When a ruling genuinely changes it, the change is recorded rather than typed into the spec’s prose. plan-rule, run with the plan folder, the fork, your choice and --quote with your own words, writes one ruling line under Rulings, a heading in the newest handoff entry that it adds when absent. With --spec-change and a --title it also writes a decision record under decisions/, adds that record to the manifest’s decision_records[] and appends one pointer line under an Amendment register heading at the end of the spec. Every check runs before the first write, so a refusal writes nothing. Adding an Amendment, Operator decision points or Rulings heading to the spec by hand draws a [SPEC AMENDMENT] advisory that names this command. The advisory never blocks the write.
  • Tasks live in the manifest — you expand the structured task list there; the machine regenerates tasks.md from it between the sentinels (your edits outside the sentinels are preserved). A task can also carry context_refs, the plan text it needs: each entry is <file>#<heading>, that section of a file in the plan folder, or ADR-NN, a decision record. plan-context with a task id prints each entry resolved, so a resuming session loads that slice instead of the whole plan.
  • Each session appends a handoff — newest-first, carrying the resume pointer. A closing session can also leave a launch prompt: a fenced block under a ### Session <N> launch prompt heading that the next session pastes to start. It should carry only what a fresh session cannot read from disk, so Brain Stem counts its words. Over 300 words, the write guard adds an advisory as you write the block, and the session-close handoff check reports it for the newest entry. The target is 200 words or fewer, and the check warns and never blocks.
  • Research and decisions accrue beside the quartet — feasibility research lands in _research/ and planning decisions in the ADR ledger, both declared in the manifest, both well before any code is written.

The plan moves through its early states as this settles: researching → planned. When the spec is firm and the tasks are real, it's ready to execute — the human gate of plan readiness.

Arming a plan for build — scoped to the project that armed it

When a plan is ready to execute you arm it: a small pointer names it as the plan under build, and from then on sessions are pointed at its spec as the authority a brief must defer to: the payload is a SPEC AUTHORITY banner, the spec's path, the current task, the active decision records and the plan-context query that prints any slice, never the spec body itself. The pointer lives inside the project's own binder directory — _projects/<spoke>/.active-plan — and every reader resolves it through one shared library. That placement is what scopes it: two projects arm at the same time without contention; a session receives the pointer for the plan armed in the project it was launched from, and nothing else; and a session in a project with nothing armed receives none, so the payload loads only into sessions launched from an armed project. An older corpus-wide pointer at the plans root is still honoured as a read-only fallback so nothing breaks on the way over, but nothing writes it any more.

Earning done — six statuses, two terminals

The lifecycle's load-bearing guard is the difference between a claim and a fact. The status model is deliberately small — six statuses, and only vocabulary the system actually enforces. A plan ends at completed (stamped with a completion timestamp on the record) or superseded. Done-integrity does not come from extra status words — it comes from the manifest being the source of truth, every human-readable list being generated from it, and independent read-only verification passes that audit the record against the artifacts on disk.

A claim, checked — not a stack of status words

Completed is a claim on the record — and the record is checkable, because the manifest is the source of truth and independent read-only passes audit it against what is actually on disk. A status you could set without any machinery behind it would be theater, so the vocabulary is held to what the machinery can actually check. Done is not something you get to declare twice. (Archival is a display-only view filter in the rendered indexes — older completed plans are tucked out of sight without any status change.)

Flat plans and master plans

Most plans are flat: one numbered folder, one quartet, one status — the right shape for a single coherent effort you can hold in one spec. Flat is the default, and a flat plan never silently grows into something bigger; you opt into more structure explicitly, only when you need it.

When an effort is too large for one spec — several workstreams or phases that each deserve their own goal, tasks, and tracking, but that have to be coordinated — it becomes a master plan that coordinates a set of sub-plans. The difference is real, not cosmetic:

The default

A flat plan

One quartet, one lifecycle status. The whole effort lives in a single spec and task list. Reach for it whenever the work fits in one coherent plan — which is most of the time.

Opt-in, for scale

A master + sub-plans

A coordinating master, plus sub-plans that are each a first-class plan — own quartet, own six-status lifecycle, numbered in execution order. Reach for it when one spec can't honestly hold the whole effort.

On disk, a master is a plan whose folder contains its sub-plans, each a full plan in its own right:

~/.claude-plans/
└── 42-rebuild-search/            the MASTER (type: master) — its own quartet
    ├── spec.md  manifest.json  tasks.md  handoff.md
    ├── 01-index-schema/          the 01 sub-plan (type: sub-plan) — its own quartet
    ├── 02-query-rewrite/         the 02 sub-plan — runs after 01 (ordering held by the master)
    └── 03-ranking/               the 03 sub-plan
                                  └ sub-plans numbered in EXECUTION order, not creation order

The invariants that keep it honest

The structure earns its keep through three strict rules — the same orchestrator-worker discipline the multi-agent research points to as what keeps decomposed work from collapsing:

  • Sub-plans never talk to each other. A sub-plan carries no dependency edge to a sibling; any cross-sub ordering is held by the master. (No sibling chatter — the coupling that sinks unsupervised multi-agent systems.)
  • Subs publish status upward only. Each sub owns and updates its own status; it never reaches into the master. The master pulls, never the reverse.
  • The master's roll-up is generated, never hand-typed. The master's summary of its sub-plans is a derived read-replica, regenerated by the librarian from each sub's real published status — the same trust class as a generated index. You cannot pencil in a sub's progress on the master.
Why it exists — and why it's exactly what the orchestrator walks

A master plan is the orchestrator-worker pattern made structural: a big effort decomposed into independently-governed pieces, coordinated from one place, with no peer-to-peer coupling. That isn't just tidy — it's executable. Because the master holds the dependency ordering and each sub is a real plan with real tasks, the orchestrator can walk a master as a dependency graph, running its sub-plans in execution order behind the same human gates. A flat plan gives you one tracked effort; a master gives you a coordinated, gate-able program of them — which is why the structure exists at all, and why you only pay for it when the scale demands it.
