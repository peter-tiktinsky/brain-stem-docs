Brain Stem builds a set of folder and file structures for you, in several places on your disk — then gathers every one of them into a single folder, named brain, using shortcuts. That gives you one way in to everything your work produces. Here is exactly what lands on day one, where each piece really lives, and why it matters. Click any entry to open it.

How to read the map

Every row carries two marks, and they answer different questions. The colour says who fills the thing. The shape says where it really lives. A row can be any combination of the two — the folder Claude maintains on its own is just as likely to sit outside the vault as inside it.

Almost nothing here is hand-typed by you. Brain Stem lays down the structure at install, and from there you supply the direction and the raw inputs while Claude generates the artifacts — filing each one in its proper place under the governance rules, and keeping the derived pages current. So read the colour as where a piece mostly comes from, not as solo authorship. The honest rule of thumb is you steer, Claude builds and maintains.

  • You direct; Claude builds it — Brain Stem ships the shell, and your direction and inputs fill it, with Claude doing the writing. Your plans, your projects, your work.
  • Claude generates & maintains it — Brain Stem ships it and Claude refreshes it on its own. These are the supporting files and indexes that keep the graph navigable and every change trackable. You never hand-edit them.
  • Ships ready to use — it exists from the moment you install. Some of these are finished files written once and then left alone, like the guide at the top of the vault and the editor config. The rest are the folders themselves — Work, Plans, Projects, Wiki — which arrive empty and ready, and fill with the rows beneath them as you work.
  • Only surfaced here — Brain Stem neither creates nor maintains it; it simply appears on this surface so you can see it alongside everything else. Exactly one thing falls in this bucket.
What install actually seeds

The brain folder is one surface among several, not the whole system. At install Brain Stem writes this folder's own contents — a guide file (CLAUDE.md), two real folders (Vault Writers/ and Bases/) and an .obsidian/ config — and it creates the other structures the system needs in their own homes elsewhere on disk. It then points at those from here with five shortcuts: Work, Projects, Wiki, Plans and Skills. A shortcut (technically a symlink) is a signpost: it shows up here for easy browsing, but the real files stay in their own home, so deleting the vault never touches your plans, projects or Library. The vault arrives nearly empty and is grown over time.

Three of the five shortcuts lead to the same place

Plans, Projects and Wiki all resolve inside the same plans structure at ~/.claude-plans — Projects and Wiki are folders within it. They are surfaced separately here so each is one click away instead of buried a level down, which is the whole point of the shortcut layer: the real hierarchy stays intact on disk, while the things you reach for most sit at the top. Work is the exception — it has its own home at ~/work.

Skills is the one pass-through

Nothing special happens to Skills. Brain Stem adds your existing global skills as a shortcut so they sit on the same surface as everything else and can be tracked against your knowledge graph. It does not create them, fill them, or maintain them — which is why it is the only row on the map in grey.

You never start a session from the vault root — you always launch from a project folder (a code repo, or a project under Work). Unfamiliar with a term here — the librarian, a shortcut, a binder? Every coined word on the site is defined on the Glossary.

What the brain folder is for

It keeps your context layer readable to you. Claude stores everything it captures as plain Markdown, so the whole store stays human-readable and navigable — no database, no locked format. Obsidian is simply the interface that makes it pleasant to browse: it opens the folder as a vault, lets you read, search, and organize its contents, and draws a knowledge graph that visualizes your assets and the relationships between them. It is optional — any editor opens the files — but it is the intended window onto your brain.

It consolidates your entire context layer into one canonical location. Everything relevant to your Claude Code work — your plans, the Library, project binders, your work area — actually lives in its own optimal home on disk; the brain folder gathers all of it in one place through the symlinks marked above. So each artifact stays where it belongs, yet the whole of your context is reviewable from a single spot. That canonical home doesn't only serve you: it lets Claude Code — or any other LLM harness you point at it — use your accumulated context as reference material. If you ever multi-home, it is the feeder for a local LLM that turns your knowledge base into a queryable asset, or for a vector-search layer that gives you another way to navigate and query everything you've built — read-only, since the governance that keeps it well-formed runs inside Claude Code (Knowledge-graph extendability).
